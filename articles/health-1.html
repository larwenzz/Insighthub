“Mental Health and Well-Being in the Modern Era: A Comprehensive Review of Challenges and Interventions” by Aminat Magomedova and Ghizal Fatima, published in Cureus in 2025

The article explains how modern life can affect people’s mental health. It focuses on problems such as stress, anxiety, depression, burnout, social isolation, excessive technology use, economic pressure, and poor sleep.

The authors explain that mental health is influenced by a combination of biological, psychological, and environmental factors. Things like brain chemistry, genetics, sleep, diet, exercise, relationships, financial situation, education, and access to healthcare can all affect a person’s well-being.

Taking care of mental health isn't just about treating depression or anxiety—it also means creating healthy lifestyles, supportive communities, reducing stigma, and making professional help easier to access.

  • Magomedova, A., & Fatima, G. (2025). Mental health and well-being in the modern era: A comprehensive review of challenges and interventions. Cureus, 17(1), e77683.
    https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.77683

MENTAL HEALTH AND THE HUMAN EXPERIENCE

Mental health is an important part of human life because it affects how people think, feel, behave, and understand the world around them. Although mental health has become a more openly discussed topic today, struggles with loneliness, grief, anxiety, fear, and emotional exhaustion have existed throughout history. Literature provides a meaningful way to explore these experiences because stories allow readers to enter the minds and lives of characters who experience struggles similar to their own.

One of the greatest strengths of literature is its ability to represent emotions that people sometimes find difficult to express. A person may struggle to explain sadness or loneliness in ordinary conversation, yet literature can transform those emotions into characters, conflicts, settings, and symbols. Through these elements, readers can recognize feelings that they may have experienced themselves. This creates a sense of understanding and reminds people that emotional struggles are part of the human experience.

Literature also demonstrates how mental health can be influenced by a person’s environment. Family problems, poverty, and discrimination, lead to a person often facing these circumstances throughout a story, allowing readers to examine how different situations shape their thoughts and decisions. Because of this, literature does not simply present mental health as an individual problem. Instead, it can encourage readers to consider the social and personal circumstances surrounding someone’s struggles.

To add, literature can challenge harmful stereotypes about mental health. When stories portray characters with emotional difficulties as complex human beings rather than simply labeling them as weak, readers can develop greater empathy. They begin to understand that a person’s struggles do not completely define who they are. A person may experience fear, sadness, or emotional confusion while still possessing strength and hope.

Reading literature can also provide comfort. Seeing a fictional character overcome hardship can give readers encouragement during difficult periods of their own lives. Literature cannot replace professional mental health support, but it can create opportunities for reflection and emotional understanding. A story can make someone feel less alone by showing that another person, even a fictional one, has experienced emotions that seem familiar.

Ultimately, literature serves as a mirror of human experience. Through characters and stories, it explores emotions that are often hidden in everyday life. Mental health becomes more understandable when people are given opportunities to discuss it without judgment. Literature helps create those opportunities by encouraging empathy, reflection, and conversation. By connecting literature with mental health, readers can gain not only a deeper understanding of stories but also a greater understanding of themselves and other people.
